The Schedule, three ways

Nobody opens a schedule for the pleasure of looking at a grid. They open it with one of four questions — what's next, when am I free, did that payment go through, and am I on time tonight — and each question wants a different screen. So here are three real answers, not three skins: a day you read top to bottom, a month you read at a glance, and a book of plans you act on.

Option AThe day spine

A single day, read down a time line. The rail is the clock, the line is the day, and the node on the line is the state of that plan.

The stages each one has to survive

A plan has a life before and after it becomes a ticket. These are the stages the real Schedule already has to render (Booking/payment states, note 27/44) — set the Moment control to see them.

What the research says

Evidence used, and where it stops

  1. Baymard measured 1,700+ hours and 311 think-aloud sessions on booking sites; a calendar that doesn't show what's inside a date makes people click blindly. One participant: "I had to click on a bunch of different dates in order to figure out what date I could even go." This is why B's month shows times in the open week instead of a bare number, and why every cell opens a real day. Baymard, 2023
  2. The same research found the picker must lead to detail, not a picture. A user tapping a date that only enlarged a photo: "you'd think that you could click on it, but now it's just enlarging the photo." B's sheet is content, not a preview. Baymard, date picker examples
  3. A short list beats a calendar when there are few dates — cap it at about ten. Calendars suit dates near the present; scrolling a long picker is "slow and unproductive". C's ribbon is a 14-slot list, not a grid; A's strip is 7. NN/g, Li 2017
  4. Progressive disclosure: two levels, no more, and the second level must be labelled with what's inside. "Designs that go beyond 2 disclosure levels typically have low usability." B has exactly two: the month, then the sheet. NN/g, Nielsen 2006
  5. Empty states have three jobs: say what the system is doing, teach, and offer a direct pathway. Never show "no records" while still loading, and never a blank container. Every option here has a written empty state, and none of them is a shrug. NN/g, Kaplan 2021
  6. Recognition beats recall: saved lists and history are cheap memory. C is a book of what you already committed to; A names the next thing so you don't count days. NN/g, Budiu 2024
  7. Chunking: visually distinct, labelled groups scan faster and are remembered better than one undifferentiated stream. This is why days are headers in A and B, and weeks are groups in C. NN/g, Moran 2016
  8. Attention leans left: 80% of fixations landed in the left half of the screen (120+ participants, 130,000+ fixations). It supports a left time gutter in A — but honestly: that study is desktop eye-tracking, not mobile calendars, and nobody has published a study on time gutters. Treat it as a convention that is cheap to read, not a proven law. NN/g, Fessenden 2017
  9. Rows are fixated hardest at their left edge (F-pattern). The time sits there in A; the state bar sits there in C. NN/g, Pernice 2017
  10. Apple HIG: 44×44 pt default targets (28 pt absolute minimum), support at least 200% text growth, and replace zoom/scale motion with fades under Reduce Motion. Also: reading order is top-to-bottom, leading-to-trailing, so put the most important thing high and leading. Here, day cells, week cells, rows and dock slots clear 44 pt; the small pills sit at 32–36 pt with 8 pt between them, which is HIG’s padding guidance for bezelled controls rather than a 44 pt face. The phone ships a Reduced switch. Apple HIG, Accessibility
  11. HIG's grouped list style separates groups with headers, footers and extra space — the platform's own answer to day grouping. Apple HIG, Lists and tables
  12. Material 3: 48×48 dp minimum touch targets, and a modal bottom sheet opens capped at 50% of screen height. B's peek is 44%, and it expands to 88% only on purpose. Material 3, bottom sheets
  13. Google Calendar itself opens on a list of upcoming events, and its "Schedule" mode is a list by day. The incumbents treat the grid as a tool, not a home. Google Calendar Help
  14. WCAG 2.2.1 (Level A): if a time limit can't be extended, you must warn before it expires and allow extension — at least 20 seconds, at least ten times. The hold card warns, and offers Pay and Extend, instead of quietly expiring. W3C WAI
  15. Scarcity has a documented failure mode: if the countdown looks invented, trust falls. So the timer only exists where a real hold expires, it counts from the server's deadline, and it never invents urgency on a normal event. NN/g, Cardello 2014
  16. Response times: 0.1 s feels direct, 1 s keeps flow, 10 s needs a progress indicator. The countdown ticks once a second and never blocks a tap. NN/g, Nielsen 1993
  17. What the market does: Fantastical ships a horizontal "DayTicker" plus full day/week/month views; Notion Calendar merges deadlines into the calendar; Amie merges todos and reshuffles the day. All three are vendor claims, not research — they show the direction, they don't prove it. Fantastical · Notion Calendar

What is not researched

  • No study on a mobile time gutter. The left-rail argument is transfer from desktop eye-tracking, plus convention. Ship it because it's cheap, not because it's proven.
  • No documented dot cap. Neither Apple nor Google says how many dots a day cell may show. Ours caps at 3 and then says how many are left — a product choice, labelled as one.
  • No HIG or Material page on calendars or agenda views. Both stop at "pickers". Everything above the picker is our judgement, defended by the studies that do exist.
  • Baymard's full date-picker guideline set is paywalled. The free summaries are what's quoted here.
States, and what each one may say

The six states, one colour each

Confirmed — a real ticket On hold — a deadline Awaiting host — no action You host — a job to do Added by you — this browser Ended — history
  • Colour is never the only signal. Hold is honey and a countdown and a Pay button; hosting is plum and the words "You host"; ended is grey and faded and says "Ended". A colour-blind reader loses nothing.
  • Each state gets the verb it deserves. Held → Pay now / Extend 10 min. Awaiting → Message the host. Confirmed → Open ticket / Directions. Hosting → Run of show / Guest list. Ended → Recap. Discover → Add to banbe Schedule / Add to calendar.
  • No state borrows another's urgency. Only a real hold counts down, and it counts down against the server's deadline, not a decorative one.
  • Awaiting is honest about waiting. It doesn't say "confirmed pending" or invent a promise; it says the host hasn't looked yet and gives you the one useful action.

What I'd ship

A, with B's month one tap away

  • Default to A. The goer's real question is "what's next and what do I owe", and a day read down a time line answers it in one look, with the state on the spine before the words.
  • Keep B's month behind A's month button — which is exactly what the prototype's month chip does in A. That's the honest hybrid, and it's one screen, not two products.
  • Borrow C's stamp for the hold. A stamped deadline on a stub is the strongest of the three at making a payment feel like an object that can be completed — worth bringing into the ticket flow, not the schedule list.
  • What to cut from today's Schedule: two mode buttons plus a month button plus a week strip plus a month toggle is four navigation controls over one list. Each option here has at most two.
  • What not to build: an "overlap" claim. Events have no end time today (note 38), so the prototype says "starts within 2 hours of a plan" — never "overlaps" — until real end times exist everywhere.

Prototype only: no backend, no auth, one fixed demo account. Everything is drawn from the app's real tokens and the real Schedule rules in src/lib/schedulePresentation.js. Photos are the project's own. Times are generated from your clock, so the demo is always "today".
