One screen, any window

v4 proved the screen: one scroll, month → day → book, no mode switch. But on an iPad it was still a 390 pt phone scaled up — the same column with bigger pixels. v5 stops scaling and starts laying out. The app is rendered at the width it is actually given and reflows at four breakpoints: the month becomes a rail, the day and book take the middle, the selected plan takes the right, and the book goes two columns when there is room. Two ways to judge it: Adaptive fills whatever window it has, and Drag the split puts banbe on one side of an iPad screen so you can move the divider the way Split View does and see the half and two-thirds states for real.

Why there is no option control any more
  • The three architectures were three answers to one question asked at three zoom levels. Month: what is my week. Day: what happens next and what does it want. Book: what comes after, and what do I still owe. A person moves through those zooms in that order anyway — so the screen should scroll through them instead of asking which one to open.
  • v2's three options each dropped something the other two had. A lost the month; B lost the day's order; C lost both. Merging B+C in v3 restored the month but left the day behind a chip — the day was still a place you went, not a lens you passed through.
  • The day spine earns its place inside the scroll, not beside it. It is the only lens that shows order — 18:30 doors, 19:00 the set, the next thing after it — and the only one where a now-line means anything. Putting it under the month also fixes v3's biggest cost: the first plan sat low, because a grid with nothing under it wastes the fold.
  • Nothing is lost in the merge. Month marks come from B. Clock reading, nodes, hold drain and the now-line come from A. The hero stub, perforation, week groups and folded history come from C. What went away is the option control, the sheet, the ribbon and every mode switch.
  • One screen is also the honest product decision. The shipped Schedule has a mode switch, a month button, a week strip and a month toggle over one list. v4 has the month, one Today chip, and a day that follows the month. That is two controls total.
What the research says

Evidence used, and where it stops

  1. Baymard measured 1,700+ hours and 311 think-aloud sessions on booking sites; a calendar that doesn't show what's inside a date makes people click blindly. One participant: "I had to click on a bunch of different dates in order to figure out what date I could even go." This is why the month shows what each day holds — shade for a ticket, a honey ring for a hold, a dashed outline for waiting, a plum ring for a door you run — instead of a bare number. Baymard, 2023
  2. The same research found the picker must lead to detail, not a picture. A user tapping a date that only enlarged a photo: "you'd think that you could click on it, but now it's just enlarging the photo." Picking a day goes straight to that day's time line — content, not a preview, and no sheet between the tap and the answer. Baymard, date picker examples
  3. A short list beats a calendar when there are few dates — cap it at about ten. Calendars suit dates near the present; scrolling a long picker is "slow and unproductive". The book is grouped and short (Today, this week, next week, later) and starts only after the day you are reading. NN/g, Li 2017
  4. Progressive disclosure: two levels, no more, and the second level must be labelled with what's inside. "Designs that go beyond 2 disclosure levels typically have low usability." The screen has month → day, and the book is not a third level but the same list continued. v2 had month → sheet → plan; that third level was the one the merges removed. NN/g, Nielsen 2006
  5. Empty states have three jobs: say what the system is doing, teach, and offer a direct pathway. Never show "no records" while still loading, and never a blank container. The Open stage writes all three: the month keeps its shape, the day says nothing is planned, and the book lists what is on this week. NN/g, Kaplan 2021
  6. Recognition beats recall: saved lists and history are cheap memory. The book is literally what you already committed to, and the month remembers the day without making you count. NN/g, Budiu 2024
  7. Chunking: visually distinct, labelled groups scan faster and are remembered better than one undifferentiated stream. The screen is one chunk per lens, and the book is chunked by week; the day is chunked by time. NN/g, Moran 2016
  8. WCAG 2.2.1 (Level A): if a time limit can't be extended, you must warn before it expires and allow extension — at least 20 seconds, at least ten times. The held stage warns and offers Pay and Extend, in the day and on the stub, instead of quietly expiring. W3C WAI
  9. Scarcity has a documented failure mode: if the countdown looks invented, trust falls. So the timer only exists where a real hold expires, it counts from the server's deadline, and it never invents urgency on a normal event. NN/g, Cardello 2014
  10. Response times: 0.1 s feels direct, 1 s keeps flow, 10 s needs a progress indicator. Picking a day redraws the day and book in one frame; the countdown ticks once a second and never blocks a tap. NN/g, Nielsen 1993
  11. Apple HIG: 44×44 pt default targets (28 pt absolute minimum), support at least 200% text growth, replace zoom/scale motion with fades under Reduce Motion, and put the most important thing high and leading. Spine rows, stub rows, dock slots and buttons clear 44 pt; month cells are 42 pt tall, the densest control on the screen, and are acceptable only because each is a full grid column. The demo ships a Reduced switch. Apple HIG, Accessibility
  12. iPadOS expects a split: lists on one side, the selected item on the other, and the right side is never empty. The wide stage does exactly that, with no Back on the detail side because nothing was pushed. Apple HIG, Layout
  13. Material 3: 48×48 dp minimum touch targets, and a modal bottom sheet opens capped at 50% of screen height. There is no sheet left to cap: the day lives in the page, in the top half of the scroll. Material 3, bottom sheets
  14. Google Calendar itself opens on a list of upcoming events, and its "Schedule" mode is a list by day. The incumbents treat the grid as a tool, not a home. Google Calendar Help
  15. What the market does: Fantastical ships a horizontal "DayTicker" plus full day/week/month views; Notion Calendar merges deadlines into the calendar; Amie merges todos and reshuffles the day. All three are vendor claims, not research — they show the direction, they don't prove it. Fantastical · Notion Calendar

What is not researched

  • No study on stacking a month, a day and a book in one phone scroll. The order is an argument from progressive disclosure and from v2's own friction, not from a measured study. Treat it as the thing to test, not the thing that is proven.
  • No study on a mobile time gutter. The left-rail argument is transfer from desktop eye-tracking, plus convention. Ship it because it's cheap, not because it's proven.
  • No documented dot cap. Neither Apple nor Google says how many dots a day cell may show. Ours caps at 3 and lets the day say the rest — a product choice, labelled as one.
  • No HIG or Material page on calendars or agenda views. Both stop at "pickers". Everything above the picker is our judgement, defended by the studies that do exist.
  • No research on stage-by-stage schedule UIs. The eight stages come from the app's own booking states (schedulePresentation.js, notes 27/44), not from a study. The claim is narrower than it sounds: a screen that changes with the state is cheaper to read than one that explains the state in a paragraph.
  • Baymard's full date-picker guideline set is paywalled. The free summaries are what's quoted here.
Stages, states, and what each one may say

The six states, one colour each

Confirmed — a real ticket On hold — a deadline Awaiting host — no action You host — a job to do Added by you — this browser Ended — history
  • Colour is never the only signal. Hold is honey and a countdown and a Pay button; hosting is plum and the words "You host"; ended is grey and faded and says "Ended". A colour-blind reader loses nothing.
  • Each stage gets the verb it deserves. Held → Pay now / Extend 10 min. Awaiting → Message the host. Confirmed → Open ticket / Directions. Day-of → Directions / Your ticket. Hosting → Run of show / Guest list. Ended → Recap. Open → Find something.
  • The stages are connected, not separate. Press Pay now on the held plan and the whole screen walks itself to Awaiting, then — the way the host's confirmation actually arrives — to Confirmed, with the day and the stub re-stamping together. That sequence is the product.
  • No stage borrows another's urgency. Only a real hold counts down, and it counts down against the deadline, not a decorative one.
  • Awaiting is honest about waiting. It doesn't say "confirmed pending" or invent a promise; it says the host hasn't looked yet and gives you the one useful action. In the month it deliberately earns no shade — waiting is not the same as busy.
  • Day-of is a different screen, not a badge. The now-line, the live node and the countdown exist so nobody scrolls to find out where they are going tonight — and the day is already the first thing under the month.

  • Motion only ever explains a change of place or a change of state. Nothing moves that you didn't touch, and nothing delays reading.
  • Changing stage is a re-staging, not a page load: the scroll keeps its place, the day re-rises in order, the month re-marks without a jump.
  • There is no lens transition to animate, because there is no lens switch. Picking a day only replaces the day and the book below a month that stays exactly where it was.
  • Under Reduce Motion the pulses become static halos, the fold becomes a cut, and the countdown still ticks — because the number is information, not decoration.
  • prefers-reduced-motion does the same automatically, so the OS setting is honoured without touching the switch.
What I'd ship

This one screen, and nothing beside it

  • Ship the scroll: month → day → book. It is the only arrangement that answers "when am I free", "what happens tonight" and "what do I still owe" without a mode switch, and the day spine finally sits where it is strongest — directly under the month, with a now-line that means something.
  • Ship the month as the only navigation. One Today chip, one set of arrows, and a day that follows whatever month you are looking at. Today's Schedule has four controls over one list; this has two.
  • Keep the stamp for the hold. A stamped deadline on a stub is the strongest way to make a payment feel like an object that can be completed — worth bringing into the ticket flow itself, not just the schedule list.
  • The stage is data, not a mode. Everything above is one list with a state per plan; the “stage” control here is a way to look at that list through one state at a time. Ship the same idea as a filter the screen can enter by itself — when a hold is expiring, the Schedule should open on it without being asked.
  • What not to build: an “overlap” claim. Events have no end time everywhere yet (note 38), so the prototype says “starts within 2 hours of a plan” — never “overlaps” — until real end times exist.
